Last spring we ran an experiment: for thirty days, every recurring meeting on the company calendar was cancelled. Not rescheduled — cancelled. If a conversation mattered, someone would have to ask for it.

## What we expected

We expected chaos. Decisions stalling, projects drifting, people quietly rebuilding the meetings one by one under new names.

## What actually happened

Almost nothing broke. The weekly status meeting was replaced by a written update that took ten minutes to write and two to read. Planning moved into a shared document where people could think before they answered.

> A meeting is the most expensive way to find out that nobody had time to prepare.

Three meetings came back, and they came back *better*:

1. **Kickoffs** — the first conversation of a new project benefits from everyone in a room.
2. **Retrospectives** — tone matters, and tone is hard to write.
3. **One-to-ones** — these were never the problem.

## What we kept

The default flipped. A meeting now needs a reason; a written update does not. On average each person got back **eleven hours a week**, and nobody has asked for them to be returned.
